AI can make teams faster and less clear at the same time

4 min read

One of the most interesting things about AI-enabled teams is that speed and clarity do not necessarily move together.

A team can do lots of work, very quickly, while becoming less aligned on what problem they actually trying to solve.

Imagine a simple product workflow.

A PM has an idea.

They use AI to turn it into a detailed PRD.

The document looks good.

An engineer takes it, uses AI to break it down, write the code and generate tests.

The team has moved from idea to implementation very quickly.

But where did the thinking happen?

Did the PM spend enough time interrogating the problem, talking to customers, reviewing numbers, thinking about all the use cases and places where the happy flows would break, or prioritising what needs to be done first?

Did the engineer understand the reasoning behind the requirements?

Were there assumptions (silently made by AI) hidden inside the PRD that none noticed?

Did anyone challenge the solution before implementation started?

This is where the interesting question begins.

AI does not have to produce bad work to create this problem.

It can produce very good-looking work.

The issue is that faster production can make it easier to skip the thinking and friction between stages.

The missing middle

Moving from an idea to implementation has always involved some friction.

  • A PM had to explain the requirements, sometimes even fight for what was important.
  • An engineer had to push back on what did not make sense technically.
  • Requirements got challenged.
  • Trade-offs were debated.
  • Someone had to explain why a particular decision mattered.

It took time.

It was frustrating, no doubt.

But that friction was doing something.

It forced people to understand the problem from different perspectives. It surfaced assumptions. It created shared context around what was actually being built and why.

AI has given us a way to avoid some of that friction.

And when that friction disappears, some of the thinking can disappear with it too.

The question is not whether we should bring the friction back. It is which parts of it are worth keeping.

And that is where the workflow itself needs to be reconsidered.

Which steps are mechanical and can be automated or outsourced to AI?

Which steps are decision points and should be retained by people?

Where does shared understanding need to exist before work moves forward?

A different way to think about speed

The goal is not to go back to the conventional way, that's probably not possible.

It is to move quickly with the right checkpoints.

For example:

HandoffWhat the checkpoint protects
Problem → PRDThe team is solving the right problem.
PRD → Technical approachThe intended behaviour is understood before implementation decisions are made.
Technical approach → BuildThe team understands the approach it is about to implement.
Build → TestThe team understands what was built and what needs verification.
Test → ShipThere is enough evidence to proceed with release.

The exact stages will vary by team.

The principle is more important.

Before the output of one stage becomes the input to the next, there should be a moment to ask:

Do we understand this well enough to move forward?

That becomes even more important when AI is doing a large part of the execution.

What those checkpoints should actually look like, what questions need to be asked and who should ask them is something I explore in a separate piece.
